# Synthesis: the demand-supply gap

## Rank on the gap, not on demand

High demand plus satisfied supply is the worst quadrant, and it is exactly where naive research points. The loudest complaint in a corpus is usually loud *because* the market is actively solving it.

### The trap, concretely

On the source run, the loudest and most-repeated complaint in the entire dataset was software bloat — solo operators paying for crew features they'll never use. It reads like an obvious #1 product.

It was the worst possible thing to build. YardBook, ServiceM8, makehay.io and Chameleon-CRM all offer free tiers aimed at exactly that operator. **The complaint was loud because the market was actively solving it.**

> Always ask: *if this pain is so common, what already serves it — and at what price?* A free incumbent kills a product idea no matter how loud the demand.

This check is the single highest-value step in the method. A run that surfaces the right complaints but skips it will confidently recommend the worst quadrant.

## Where the opportunity actually sits

**The seam is what paid incumbents explicitly don't do.** The most valuable sentence found on the source run was a LawnSite comment: *Jobber at $49/month handles quoting, scheduling and invoicing — but it does not calculate actual job costs.* One sentence naming a paid tool's boundary is worth more than fifty upvotes of general complaining.

**Watch for the layer beneath the tool.** The best insight on the source run was that owners *"switch tools twice and keep the same chaos both times because the record standard never existed."* The gap wasn't software — it was the standard the software assumes you already have. Standards, SOPs, and pricing math don't get commoditized to free the way trackers do.

## Score each candidate

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Demand evidence | How many independent, credible, unprompted sources? |
| Supply saturation | How many products exist? **Any of them free?** |
| Price ceiling | What do comparable things actually sell for? |
| Build effort | Days, or months? |
| Defensibility | Could a competitor clone it from the sales page? |

Run the supply check as actual research, not recall — search for what exists, find its pricing page, and note whether there's a free tier. An idea's ranking should move when this step finds a free incumbent.

## Report structure

Write to `<niche>-opportunities.md`. Follow this shape:

```markdown
# [Niche] Digital Products — Demand vs. Supply

**Scope:** [operator population]
**Researched:** [date] · [sources, named]

## The one finding that drives everything

[The structural insight, if the run found one — the thing that reframes the
category. Support it with 2-4 verbatim quotes from independent sources,
attributed. Then state the implication for what to build.]

## Most promising products

| # | Product | The gap (what they say vs what exists) | Price | Why it wins | Main risk |
|---|---------|----------------------------------------|-------|-------------|-----------|

## What to avoid (and why it looks tempting)

| Product | Why it fails |
|---------|--------------|

## Pricing context

[Bands by marketplace. Note positioning signals — e.g. repeated explicit
demand for one-time cost over another subscription.]

## Evidence quality — read before acting

- **Strong:** [what converged, across which independent sources]
- **Caveat — vendor noise:** [which threads were contaminated, whose comments
  you weighted and why]
- **Gap:** [what you could not verify and why]
- **Soft:** [what is inferred rather than measured]

## Recommended move

[Sequencing. Usually: a fast cheap product to prove the audience buys, then the
premium one sold to that list.]
```

Two sections carry disproportionate weight:

**"What to avoid, and why it looks tempting"** is as valuable as the recommendations and is the part naive research always omits. A reader who only sees the winners will independently rediscover the trap.

**Evidence quality** is what makes the report actionable rather than merely confident. Name the contaminated threads. Say which figures are inferred from listing prices rather than revenue — no marketplace publishes real sales data. Say that community data skews toward the vocal and the newly-struggling; operators who quietly solved a problem aren't posting.

End by telling the user exactly which numbers need manual spot-checking, and how.
